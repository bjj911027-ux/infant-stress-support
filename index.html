About the science

What is this based on?

This guide draws on developmental research about stress in early childhood.

Stress does not affect every child in the same way. What happens depends on many things, including how intense or long-lasting the stress is, how predictable the situation feels, the child's individual sensitivity, responsive caregiving, and the support available around the family.

That is why this website does not try to label a stressful event or diagnose your baby from a few questions.

Sources behind this guide

  • Primary science source National Scientific Council on the Developing Child (2026). Finding the Balance: Transforming How We Think About the Body’s Response to Stress in Early Childhood.
  • Foundational source Shonkoff, J. P., Garner, A. S., et al. (2012). The Lifelong Effects of Early Childhood Adversity and Toxic Stress.
  • Conceptual source McLaughlin, K. A., Sheridan, M. A., & Lambert, H. K. (2014). Childhood Adversity and Neural Development: Deprivation and Threat as Distinct Dimensions of Early Experience.
About this guide

A note about this guide

This resource is for everyday and ongoing stressful situations. It does not diagnose toxic stress or measure your baby's stress response.

If you are worried about your baby's immediate health or safety, contact an appropriate medical or emergency service.

If you have concerns about ongoing stress or your baby's development, consider talking with your child's health care provider.
